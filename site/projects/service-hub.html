Service Hub, a trusted home services marketplace for adults 65+ and their caregivers

Overview

01

Service Hub is a mobile home-services marketplace built for adults 65 and older and for the adult children who help them. Seniors use it to book plumbers, electricians, handymen, and other local professionals, while their family caregivers use it to follow what is happening in their parent's home, even from another city.

The product is organised around a single idea: trust is not a feature to be added later but the architecture of the product itself. Every screen, badge, and piece of copy was tested against one question. Does this make a senior feel safer letting a stranger into their home?

Design principle: A senior should feel as confident letting a Service Hub pro into their home as they would with a neighbor's recommendation.

The Problem

02

According to AARP, 75% of adults over 50 want to remain in their current home as long as possible, yet no major home-services app is built for them. The dominant platforms were designed for younger, tech-confident users, and the 65+ demographic was never the target, neither in the interaction model, the trust layer, nor the pricing structure.

At the same time, older Americans lose an estimated $81.5 billion per year to financial fraud, with home-repair scams among the most common vectors. The existing apps do not solve this problem; in many cases, they compound it.

01

Designed for the wrong user

Hourly bids, in-app chat, and digital dispute forms assume a user who is fluent with mobile apps, and many seniors are not. They want a phone call, a clear price, and a person on the other end of the line rather than a chatbot.

02

No real trust infrastructure

TaskRabbit holds a 1.5-star rating with the Better Business Bureau, with 364 complaints logged in the last three years. Background checks are shallow, and workmanship guarantees are written to protect the platform rather than the customer, leaving seniors exposed.

03

No place for the caregiver

Adult children often help make service decisions for aging parents, yet they have essentially no visibility into what happens inside that home today: no booking record, no professional's photo, and no way to confirm whether the job actually went through.

Market & Users

03

The demographic wave is already here. By 2030, 71.6 million Americans will be 65 or older, up from 61.2 million today. Their homeownership rate of 79% sits 14 points above the national average, and they control a growing share of residential spending.

The owner-occupied remodeling market is projected to reach $509 billion in 2025, with adults 55 and older accounting for more than 56% of that expenditure. With mortgage rates holding near 6.5%, existing-home sales have fallen to a 30-year low, meaning seniors are not moving; they are staying put and investing in upgrades. AARP's AgeTech Collaborative has grown to more than 600 companies and 90 venture partners since 2021, suggesting that capital is beginning to follow the demographics. The window for a category-defining senior home-services platform is narrow, somewhere between two and four years.

Competitor landscape

Every major competitor addresses part of the need, but none serves this user well.

TaskRabbit An app-only marketplace with no phone booking or human support line, a 1.5-star BBB rating, hundreds of unresolved complaints, and generic trust signals.
Angi and HomeAdvisor A lead-generation model in which the platform sells user contact information to service providers. Both settled with the FTC in 2025 over misleading practices, and vetting remains inconsistent.
GoGoGrandparent A phone concierge service for seniors built on a strong premise, but with a thin home-maintenance offering, high service fees, and limited national coverage.
Papa Distributed through health insurers rather than sold directly to seniors. Faced abuse allegations reported by Bloomberg in 2023 and was not built for self-serve home-repair bookings.

The two people the product serves

Research pointed to two users who share one job: getting a trusted professional into the home without anyone losing control of the process.

M

Margaret, 71, Senior Homeowner

Portland, OR  ·  Widowed  ·  Aging in place

Goals

  • Find someone she can trust in her home without asking her son for help every time
  • Stay in charge of her own decisions
  • Know the price before the work starts and have a clear paper trail

Pain Points

  • Does not want to feel watched or supervised by family
  • Worries about being overcharged or pressured into extra work
  • Gets anxious when an app shows vague status messages
D

David, 47, Adult Caregiver Son

San Francisco, CA  ·  Manages remotely

Goals

  • Know who is going into his mother's home, and when
  • Step in fast if something looks wrong, without overriding her
  • See billing clearly so he can spot odd charges early

Pain Points

  • Coordinates from 600 miles away with no live visibility today
  • Fears a pro may not show up or may charge for work not done
  • Worries any oversight feature will upset his mother

Design Principles

04

Six principles guided every decision shaping the product. Each emerged directly from the research and the competitor analysis.

01

Trust is the architecture

Every screen, badge, and line of copy was evaluated against a single question: does this make a senior feel safer letting this person into their home?

02

Specificity over assertion

A claim such as "Insured, $1M liability, OR PLB-28841" can be independently verified, while "Verified" cannot. Trust badges should cite concrete details rather than rely on vague claims.

03

Senior autonomy first

Every caregiver feature is opt-in and controlled by the senior, who sets the permission while the caregiver operates strictly within it.

04

Design for two users at once

Every feature was tested against both personas. Any feature that served the senior while leaving the caregiver unsupported, or vice versa, was redesigned.

05

Prevent errors, do not just fix them

Fixed pricing, price confirmation before booking, and a short undo window collectively remove the conditions that produce mistakes, in keeping with Nielsen's fifth usability heuristic.

06

Lower the decision cost

Fewer choices at each step, guided by Hick's Law, pre-vetted recommendations in place of open bidding, quick-tap common issues instead of free text, and a one-tap option to rebook the same provider.

Dual-User Architecture

05

The product's core idea is two users pursuing one shared job. Each receives an interface tailored to their specific needs, while both sides rest on a single shared trust layer underneath.

The senior interface Role selection opens the experience, followed by OTP login by phone number so no password is required. A three-step profile setup opens with a privacy note on step one. Large tap targets accompany icon-led service categories, and provider lists include face photos, verified badges, and review counts. A "Call the pro" button appears before any commitment, a fixed price is shown before booking, and payment is charged only once the senior marks the job done. A day-of reminder phone call arrives at 9 AM, and cancellations carry a two-minute undo window.
The caregiver interface A separate onboarding path begins with "I am helping a family member," followed by an access-request flow that only connects once the senior approves. The dashboard surfaces the senior's home name, address, and recent job statistics, along with live status on every booking and provider photos and badges visible on each job card. Pricing is displayed transparently, as in "$200 ($40 per hour)." Caregivers can approve or cancel pending bookings, view an alerts tab for items requiring human judgement, and operate entirely within the permissions set by the senior.

Key Design Decisions

06

Four decisions define the product, and each maps directly to a specific finding from research, a competitor gap, or a rule drawn from established usability work.

01

Specific credentials instead of generic badges

An early version displayed "Insured" as a plain label. The final version shows "Insured, $1M liability" and "Licensed, OR PLB-28841," credentials that seniors can actually look up. This is how genuine word-of-mouth trust operates: it names a specific person and a specific, verifiable fact.

02

Caregiver approval is opt in and senior controlled

The "Require their approval to book" toggle is off by default and framed as a choice the senior makes. Seniors who fear losing independence will not adopt a product that feels like surveillance, so framing the feature as a gift from the senior to the caregiver removes that barrier.

03

Fixed price shown before the user commits

Open-ended hourly rates remain the primary mechanism in home-repair fraud against seniors. The booking flow therefore displays a fixed or estimated total before the confirm button, and the caregiver dashboard shows both the total and the rate type, as in "Est. total $200 ($40 per hour)," allowing a family member to sanity-check the figure from anywhere.

04

Two-minute undo window after cancellation

Cancelling a booking is a stressful moment, so the undo screen displays a live countdown directly beneath the primary action. The Peak-End Rule from behavioural research holds that the final moments of an experience shape how the whole event is remembered, and a generous exit ramp keeps that memory calm.

User Flow

07

The primary flow spans first launch, role selection, sign-in, service discovery, booking, and confirmation. Secondary branches handle the caregiver path, reschedules, cancellations, and empty states in which no providers are available nearby.

Visual Design

08

The visual language is built around two values: warmth and legibility. A cream background (#FCF9F6) is paired with a deep burnt-brown primary (#702B00), producing a palette that reads as domestic rather than clinical. All interactive elements meet WCAG AA contrast requirements, and body copy and category labels are sized larger than typical mobile defaults so the app remains easy to read in daylight and for aging eyes.

High Fidelity Screens

09

Twenty-six screens trace the complete product, beginning with onboarding and moving through booking, post-booking management, cancellation, review, messaging, the caregiver flow, and edge states where no providers are available nearby.

User Testing

10

Testing was conducted in person with three participants: two seniors and one caregiver. Each session used ten decision-ready questions covering trust, pricing, status clarity, and the circumstances that would cause a participant to stop using the app.

"Pending means it didn't go through? Did something go wrong? Has my card been charged?"

Patricia Adeyemi, 70 Senior, Toronto, first-time app user

"Before anyone comes to my house I want to speak to them. Is he going to pick up or is it like a message?"

Testing Insights

11

Four findings stood out across the sessions, each pointing to a clear change in the product, a decision worth preserving, or a gap worth designing next.

01

"Pending" status causes anxiety

All three users misread "Pending" as a failed booking; two attempted to go back and start over, and one showed clear signs of stress. Action: change the label to "Awaiting David's confirmation" and add a supporting line beneath it, "David usually confirms within two hours."

02

"Call the pro" button needs one more line

Both seniors asked the same question without being prompted: will the pro actually pick up, or will the call go to voicemail? Action: add a single supporting line beneath the button, "David usually responds within 15 minutes."

03

Specific trust credentials work

James, the sceptic, said he would google the licence number to verify it, while Patricia, a first-time app user, said the specific badge made her feel "like someone checked him." Two very different users produced the same trust outcome. Do not simplify the badge copy: the specificity is the mechanism.

04

Arrival confirmation is the biggest missing feature

Sandra raised this without being prompted. She wants a push notification that reads "Elena arrived at 12:04 PM," paired with a one-tap call option in case something feels wrong. That single notification directly addresses the primary caregiver fear of not knowing whether the pro actually showed up.

Outcomes & Reflection

12

Service Hub progressed from a blank page to a 26-screen iOS prototype across four rounds of revision. Every screen can be traced back to a research finding, a competitor gap, or a testing insight, and no decision was made on taste alone.

The dual-customer approach held up under scrutiny. Designing two interfaces that share a single trust layer is harder than designing one, and it is also precisely what distinguishes the product from every competitor on the list. The emphasis on trust specificity held up as well; generic "verified" badges did almost nothing in testing, while licence numbers, insurance carriers, and renewal dates did the real work.

The most useful lesson concerned autonomy. The caregiver approval toggle could have shipped as a default-on safety feature, but that approach would have failed; seniors would have felt supervised and walked away. Reframing the toggle as an opt-in gift from the senior to the caregiver transformed the same feature from a reason to reject the product into a reason to trust it.

The next steps are clear: ship the four fixes surfaced in testing, build arrival confirmation, run a second round of testing with six participants, design the provider side of the marketplace, and adapt the experience for Canada, where 78% of seniors own their home and platform competition remains lighter.
